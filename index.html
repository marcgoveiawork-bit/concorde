Concorde

Aérospatiale & British Aircraft Corporation

Mach 2. 60,000 feet.

Twenty built, fourteen in airline service, twenty-seven years carrying passengers at twice the speed of sound. Scroll on to explore the engineering behind the longest-serving supersonic airliner.

The airframe

Painted to keep cool

Most of the structure is an aluminium alloy, Hiduminium RR58, first developed for jet-engine parts. At Mach 2 friction heats the nose to about 127 °C, so the white paint is far more reflective than an ordinary airliner's, to stop the sun adding any more.

The design

Drawn in two languages

Britain and France agreed by treaty in 1962 to build it together, with drawing offices in Filton and Toulouse. The French prototype, 001, first flew from Toulouse on 2 March 1969; the British 002 followed from Filton on 9 April.

The drooping nose

Concorde lands nose-high, so the pilots lowered the nose to see the runway: 5° for taxi and takeoff, 12.5° for landing. In cruise it rose flush and a glass visor slid up over the windscreen.

Exterior tour, 2 of 5

An ogee delta wing

No tailplane and no flaps. Six elevons on the trailing edge handle pitch and roll, and at low speed the sharp, curved leading edge rolls the air into strong vortices that keep the wing lifting at steep angles.

Exterior tour, 3 of 5

Intakes that do the work

Movable ramps inside each intake slow Mach 2 air below the speed of sound before it reaches the engine. At cruise about 63% of the forward push comes from pressure in the intakes; the engine itself contributes only around 8% directly.

Exterior tour, 4 of 5

Four Olympus 593s

Built by Rolls-Royce and Snecma, each makes 38,050 lbf with reheat. Reheat was lit for takeoff and to push through the sound barrier, then switched off: Concorde cruised at Mach 2 without it.

Exterior tour, 5 of 5

Fuel as ballast

As speed builds, the wing's centre of lift slides aft. Rather than hold the nose up with drag-making elevons, the flight engineer pumped fuel back to tanks in the tail during the climb, and forward again before slowing down.

Takeoff

Four engines in reheat

For the takeoff roll all four engines light their afterburners: about 152,000 lbf of thrust between them, and a roar that made Concorde famous at every airport it visited.

Early estimates put the programme at about £70 million. It came to around £1.3 billion, shared by two governments, and only British Airways and Air France ever flew it.

Climb

Up to 60,000 feet

Higher than any other airliner. From there the sky overhead turns a deep, dark blue and passengers could see the curve of the Earth.

Cruise

Mach 2, and a little longer

At cruise the heat stretches the airframe by several inches. On the flight deck a gap opened beside the flight engineer's panel, and one engineer famously tucked his cap into it on a final flight, trapped there when the metal cooled.

1976 to 2003

The last of its kind

Air France ended its flights in May 2003 and British Airways in October. On 26 November 2003 the last one built flew home to Filton. No airliner has carried passengers faster than sound since.

Twice the speed of sound

CONCORDE

LONDON · PARIS · NEW YORK

MACH 2

Built by Britain and France and flown by British Airways and Air France from 1976 to 2003, Concorde crossed the Atlantic in about three and a half hours at 60,000 feet, above the weather and almost everything else in the sky. Four Rolls-Royce/Snecma Olympus engines, an ogee delta wing, and a transatlantic record no airliner has beaten.
